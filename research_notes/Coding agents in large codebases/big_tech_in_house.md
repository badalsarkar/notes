## Google: what was built, how it is integrated, what the numbers mean, and what failed

### Takeaway
Google's best evidence is its migration experience report, which describes an expert-run pipeline with human review rather than an autonomous agent. The 75% headline has only a one-line definition, "AI-generated and approved by engineers", and it should not be read as a continuation of the earlier character-share series without confirmation.

### Cited Findings

**Share of code, in date order, with each definition**

- June 2024: About 50% of code characters were completed with AI assistance, defined as accepted AI-suggested characters divided by manually typed plus accepted AI-suggested characters, with copy-pasted characters excluded from the denominator. The authors call this a rough measure of impact. **[page]** — [Google Research blog, 6 June 2024](https://research.google/blog/ai-in-software-engineering-at-google-progress-and-the-path-ahead/)
- June 2024: The acceptance rate was 37%, defined as accepted suggestions divided by suggestions shown for more than 750 milliseconds while the user is not typing. **[page]** — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/ai-in-software-engineering-at-google-progress-and-the-path-ahead/)
- October 2024: Pichai said on the Q3 earnings call that more than a quarter of new code is generated by AI. **[search]** — [Fortune, 30 October 2024](https://www.fortune.com/2024/10/30/googles-code-ai-sundar-pichai)
- April 2026: Pichai said 75% of all new code at Google is AI-generated and approved by engineers, up from 50% the previous fall. He also said a complex migration ran six times faster than was possible a year earlier with engineers alone, and he described the workflow as "truly agentic". **[search]** — [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91531519/google-ceo-says-75-of-the-companys-code-is-ai-generated); [Semafor, 24 April 2026](https://www.semafor.com/article/04/24/2026/google-ceo-says-75-of-companys-new-code-is-ai-generated)
- Secondary commentary notes that Google has not published the acceptance rate or the rework behind the 75% figure, and this commentary is itself of low authority. **[search]** — [DevOps.com](https://devops.com/google-ceo-says-75-of-new-code-is-ai-generated)

**Code migration paper (Nikolov et al., arXiv 2501.06972, January 2025)**

- The authors state that the paper is an experience report that makes no comparisons and tests no hypotheses. **[page]** — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06972)
- Ads int32-to-int64 ID migration: The codebase has more than 500 million lines, and the change touched tens of thousands of locations across thousands of files. Engineers estimated about a 50% reduction in total time against a comparable non-LLM effort, and about 80% of landed modifications were fully AI-authored, measured by a per-character diff between the first LLM snapshot and the committed version. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)
- JUnit3-to-JUnit4 migration: The migration covered 5,359 files and more than 149,000 lines in about three months, and about 87% of generated code was committed without changes. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)
- Joda-to-java.time migration: Tech leads estimated about 89% time saved on small clusters, and that migration was still in progress when the paper was written. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)
- Context retrieval: Engineers combine Code Search, Kythe cross-references and custom scripts, start from a seed such as a protobuf field, follow references up to three levels, and filter false positives with regex and AST checks. The toolkit then adds related tests, interfaces and dependencies. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)
- Model and feedback: The system uses a Gemini version fine-tuned on internal code. Builds and unit tests run on each change, failures go to a model trained on failed-build and fix pairs, several prompt variants run per file group, and candidates are ranked by closeness to example diffs. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)
- Workflow: An expert engineer triggers the run, checks the output and shards the changes to code owners. Rollout is human-driven and gradual. In the JUnit migration, review speed was the bottleneck, so generation was deliberately throttled. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)
- Admitted limits: Simple prompting worked only for the simplest migrations. Humans reverted at least some model changes in most ID-migration cases. Call graphs missed Guice-wired dependencies, tests that check multiple flags were hard to clean, tooling upkeep is an ongoing cost, and long-term quality effects are unknown. Extra LLM planning often added complexity without being needed. **[page]** — [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972)

**Critique (code review)**

- More than 8% of code review comments were addressed with AI assistance in June 2024, which supersedes the 7.5% reported in 2023. **[page]** — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/ai-in-software-engineering-at-google-progress-and-the-path-ahead/); **[search]** — [Google paper on resolving review comments](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/7525.pdf)
- The AutoCommenter study estimates a comment-resolution rate of about 40% from a manually reviewed sample of comment-disappearance cases. This measures resolution of automated coding-practice comments, so it is not comparable to the 8% figure. **[search]** — [arXiv 2405.13565](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13565v1)

### Inferences
- The series 25%, 50% and 75% probably mixes or redefines metrics, because Google already reported about 50% of characters in June 2024, four months before it reported "more than a quarter" of new code. Both statements can only be true if they measure different things.
- "Approved by engineers" is a weak filter as a definition, because all code at Google passes review. It therefore adds no quality information.
- The published evidence on review supports review capacity as the binding constraint, since Google throttled generation to match it.
- The migration figures describe selected, repetitive, well-validated tasks and should not be generalised to ordinary feature work.

### Gaps
- I could not open Pichai's primary blog post, because a guessed URL returned 404 and Fast Company returned 403. Its exact wording and the source of the 50% "last fall" figure should be checked.
- I found no reliable public source on internal use of Jules, Gemini CLI or Antigravity, or on the sandboxing of any internal Google agent.
- I found no public definition of how Google attributes a line of code as AI-generated in the 75% figure.
- I found no source for the "well over 30%" April 2025 figure beyond a single secondary account, so it is omitted from the series.

## Meta: what was built, how it is integrated, what the numbers mean, and what failed

### Takeaway
Meta's strongest public evidence concerns LLM test generation, where its papers give real denominators and report modest true-positive yields. Its agent adoption numbers come from leaked targets and an undefined CFO metric, and they describe goals and inputs rather than outcomes.

### Cited Findings

**Test generation research (company authors)**

- TestGen-LLM (February 2024): On Instagram Reels and Stories, 75% of generated test cases built correctly, 57% passed reliably, and 25% increased coverage. In test-a-thons on Instagram and Facebook, 11.5% of classes to which the tool was applied were improved, and engineers accepted 73% of recommendations for production. The abstract gives no absolute counts. **[page]** — [arXiv 2402.09171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171)
- ACH (January 2025): The system was applied to 10,795 Android Kotlin classes across 7 platforms, producing 9,095 mutants and 571 privacy-hardening tests, and engineers accepted 73% of the tests. **[search]** — [arXiv 2501.12862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12862)
- ACH blog (30 September 2025): The trial ran October to December 2024 on privacy use cases, mainly in Kotlin. 36% of tests were judged privacy-relevant, with the denominator implied rather than stated. Equivalent-mutant detection had precision 0.79 and recall 0.47, rising to 0.95 and 0.96 with simple preprocessing such as stripping comments. **[page]** — [Engineering at Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2025/09/30/security/llms-are-the-key-to-mutation-testing-and-better-compliance/)
- A summary of the ACH work reports kill rates of 12 to 15% against 2.0 to 2.4% for TestGen-LLM on the same classes, which is the authors' own comparison. I did not confirm this on the page. **[search]** — [Engineering at Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2025/09/30/security/llms-are-the-key-to-mutation-testing-and-better-compliance/)
- Just-in-Time catching tests (30 January 2026): Among 22,126 generated tests, code-change-aware methods gave a 4x improvement in candidate catches over hardening tests and 20x over coincidentally failing tests. Rule-based and LLM assessors cut human review load by 70%. Of 41 candidate catches reported to engineers, 8 were confirmed true positives (about 20%), and 4 of those 8 would have led to serious failures, which is about 10% of the 41 candidates. **[page]** — [arXiv 2601.22832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832)
- The Harden and Catch paper separates hardening tests, which pass when generated, from catching tests, which are meant to fail and reveal bugs before code lands. **[search]** — [arXiv 2504.16472](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16472)

**Targets and adoption**

- A leaked document, reported by Business Insider and seen by me only through a Breitbart relay, says one creation organization set a first-half 2026 goal of 65% of engineers writing more than 75% of committed code with AI help. It also describes 80% adoption of tools such as DevMate, Metamate and Gemini among mid to senior engineers, and 55% of code changes in central product organizations being Agent-Assisted. **[search]** — [Breitbart relaying Business Insider, 27 March 2026](https://www.breitbart.com/tech/2026/03/27/mark-zuckerbergs-meta-pushes-to-become-an-ai-native-company/)
- A blog describing a companywide "75% mandate" with performance-review consequences goes beyond what the reporting supports, and it should not be used. **[search]** — [Happycapy guide](https://happycapyguide.com/blog/meta-75-percent-ai-coding-mandate-engineers-layoffs-zuckerberg-2026)
- Zuckerberg forecast that within 12 to 18 months most code going toward these efforts would be written by AI, and this is a forecast limited to specific agent efforts. **[search]** — [Yahoo Tech](https://tech.yahoo.com/ai/articles/mark-zuckerberg-predicts-ai-write-213851853.html)

**Outcome claims and organisation**

- On the Q4 2025 call in January 2026, CFO Susan Li said output per engineer had risen 30% since early 2025, mostly from AI coding agents, and that power users saw an 80% year-over-year gain. **[page]** — [Computerworld, 10 April 2026](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4157175/meta-moves-fast-toward-a-world-where-ai-builds-the-software.html); **[search]** — [Axios, 29 January 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/01/29/zuckerberg-ai-work-meta)
- A Reuters-reported memo describes a new Applied AI Engineering unit with mandatory transfers, and it states a goal of agents doing most of the building, testing and shipping while humans monitor them. This is an aspiration, and Computerworld did not independently verify the memo. Forrester's Charlie Dai said in the same article that agent-led engineering is achievable today only in tightly scoped domains. **[page]** — [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4157175/meta-moves-fast-toward-a-world-where-ai-builds-the-software.html)
- Chief AI Officer Alexandr Wang reportedly said an agent swarm outperformed 100 engineers, qualified as "on specific tasks" with a strong evaluation system. The source is low-authority and no data were given. **[search]** — [Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/meta-ai-agent-swarm-outperforms-engineers/)
- Fortune reported in December 2024 that Metamate incorporated OpenAI's GPT-4 alongside Llama. This is dated and may be superseded. **[search]** — [Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2024/12/03/meta-openai-gpt-4-llama-coding-tool)

### Inferences
- The most useful Meta number for a sceptical reader is 8 of 41, because it has a clear denominator and shows that an automated bug-catching system still has about an 80% false-positive rate at the engineer stage after filtering.
- The two 73% figures are different metrics on different systems, and the repeated value should not be read as a stable acceptance rate.
- "Output per engineer" is undefined in the sources I read. One secondary analysis argues it likely counts volume, but this is an interpretation and Meta has not confirmed it.
- Adoption targets create an incentive to attribute more work to AI, which weakens any later self-reported share of AI code.

### Gaps
- I found no source for "Capture the Flag" tests at Meta in two targeted searches. The term may be misremembered or may refer to something I did not surface, so the report should not assert it.
- I found no primary engineering description of DevMate's architecture, context retrieval or sandboxing.
- I did not read the Business Insider original, so the leaked targets rest on a relay from a partisan outlet and should be checked before quoting.

## Microsoft and GitHub: what was built, how it is integrated, what the numbers mean, and what failed

### Takeaway
The dotnet/runtime record is the strongest evidence in this field, because the denominators, comparison groups and failures are all stated and the underlying pull requests are public. It shows a 67.9% success rate against 84.9% for humans, gains that depended on environment preparation, and a heavier review burden than for human PRs.

### Cited Findings

All figures in this block come from one post unless stated otherwise: [Stephen Toub, "Ten Months with Copilot Coding Agent in dotnet/runtime", .NET Blog, 23 March 2026](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/dotnet/ten-months-with-cca-in-dotnet-runtime/) **[page]**. The window is 19 May 2025 to 22 March 2026, and the author calls the data directional.

- **Definition:** Success rate is merged divided by merged plus closed, which excludes open PRs. CCA PRs are created only at the explicit request of a person with maintainer rights.
- **Volume:** CCA opened 878 PRs in dotnet/runtime, of which 535 merged, 253 closed and 90 remained open. Merged PRs added over 95,000 lines and removed 31,000. CCA made up 22.2% of Microsoft-originated PRs by count (878 of 3,960), which the author notes measures count rather than effort.
- **Comparison:** Success was 87.1% for Microsoft humans, 79.7% for community contributors, 84.9% for humans combined, 85.9% for bots and 67.9% for CCA. The author stresses that the populations differ and that humans take on more complex work.
- **Other repositories:** Across seven repositories CCA opened 2,963 PRs with 1,885 merged, a 68.6% rate on that repository set. Individual rates were microsoft/aspire 64.8%, dotnet/roslyn 74.7%, dotnet/aspnetcore 71.2%, dotnet/efcore 69.2%, dotnet/extensions 79.7% and the C# MCP SDK 77.3%.
- **Reverts:** 3 of 535 merged CCA PRs (0.6%) were reverted against 33 of 4,251 non-CCA merged PRs (0.8%), which the author calls not statistically meaningful. Reverts were identified by a title search, so this is a weak quality measure.
- **By task:** Success was 84.7% for removal and cleanup, 75.6% for testing, 69.7% for refactoring, 69.4% for bug fixes, 64.5% for features and 54.5% for performance work.
- **By size:** Success was 80.0% for 1 to 10 lines, 64.0% for 101 to 500 lines and 71.9% for over 1,000 lines. The author says task scope matters more than size.
- **Preparation:** Success was 38.1% before the setup changes and 69% afterwards. At first there was no `copilot-instructions.md` and the sandbox firewall blocked NuGet feeds, so the agent could not build or test. Monthly success was 41.7% in May 2025 (n=24) and about 70% from December 2025 onward (71.2% in January 2026, n=182).
- **Build and test feedback:** The agent runs in a sandboxed Linux x64 environment. It cannot compile or test Windows, macOS, iOS, Android or ARM64-specific paths, and humans must often relay CI failures back to it by hand. A custom skill can invoke the EgorBot benchmarking bot through a PR comment, and the results re-invoke the agent.
- **Human intervention:** 396 PRs with human commits had 86.2% success, against 55.1% for 482 PRs with none. The post reports an overall intervention rate of 52.3%, about five times the 10.3% baseline for human PRs.
- **Review burden:** Merged CCA PRs had a median of 10 comments against 7 for humans, and 24.5% against 15.5% had 21 or more comments. Only 2.2% merged without written comments. jkotas and stephentoub together wrote 36% of human feedback. A complex review takes 30 to 60 minutes, and the author says one person can generate PRs faster than a team can review them.
- **Closed PRs:** Of 253 closed PRs, 44% were auto-closed or abandoned drafts, 16% took the wrong approach, 13% were superseded and 8% had a rejected design. Categories were assigned by Claude Opus 4.6. The post counts 83 of 658 closures as attributable to output quality (13%).
- **Admitted failures:** Native C++ and CoreCLR work was the least successful area. The agent made unverified performance claims, showed weak architectural judgment on API shape and binary size (PR #120633 took more than 20 commits and was closed), did the minimum requested work, and could produce tests that validate existing incorrect behaviour. The author does not analyse compute or CI cost.

**Other Microsoft evidence**

- A TypeScript-to-Rust port of the Copilot agent runtime converted about 430,000 lines into about 800,000 lines over 14.5 weeks, using about $120,000 in tokens and about three weeks of one developer's time. The port caused a few dozen regressions, and the largest file (session.ts, over 30,000 lines) took 25 hours and 15 child sessions. Toub said this does not mean every large TypeScript program should move to Rust, and the 15.9x speedup comes from a single workload. **[page]** — [The Register, 18 September 2026](https://www.theregister.com/devops/2026/09/18/microsoft-agentically-ports-copilot-runtime-to-rust-for-120k/5297549)
- GitHub's post says agents wrote most of the code across 128 PRs. **[search]** — [GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/migrating-the-github-copilot-runtime-to-rust-using-copilot/)
- Nadella said at LlamaCon in late April 2025 that 20 to 30% of code in Microsoft's repositories is "written by software". No measurement method was given, and the statement is an estimate from a fireside chat. **[search]** — [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-microsoft-meta-code-written-by-ai/); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/microsofts-ceo-reveals-that-ai-writes-up-to-30-percent-of-its-code-some-projects-may-have-all-of-its-code-written-by-ai)
- Kevin Scott's 95% of code by 2030 is a forecast rather than a measurement. **[search]** — [Cyber Daily](https://www.cyberdaily.au/digital-transformation/12028-microsoft-says-20-30-of-its-code-is-written-by-ai)
- Secondary coverage conflicts on language differences, so the language claim should not be used without the transcript. One summary reports the most success with C++ and Python, and another reports better results for Python than C++. **[search]** — [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-microsoft-meta-code-written-by-ai/)

### Inferences
- The headline lesson is about verification, since the agent's weakest areas are those it cannot build or test in its sandbox.
- Success without human commits (55.1%) is a better estimate of autonomous performance than the blended 67.9%, although selection effects run in both directions.
- The rise from about 40% to about 70% cannot be attributed to preparation alone, because the models also changed over the period. The author's own conclusion that preparation matters more than the model is an opinion and not a controlled result.
- The published ratios do not all reconcile from the counts I read. 396 of 878 is 45.1% (my calculation) rather than the 52.3% intervention rate, and 86.2% of 396 plus 55.1% of 482 gives about 607 merges (my calculation) rather than 535. The denominators of those subset rates are therefore different from the PR counts shown, and the post should be checked before these figures are combined.
- A firecrawl.dev write-up reports "41.7% to a sustained 71%", which blends the monthly series with a single month. The primary figures should be used instead. **[search]** — [Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/ai-software-factory)

### Gaps
- I did not recompute any figure from GitHub, because the `gh` CLI is unauthenticated in this environment. The PR record is public, so a reader can recompute it.
- I could not verify the May 2025 public criticism of agent PRs on dotnet/runtime (often associated with PR #115762), because search hit a rate limit and `gh` returned 401. No claim is made about the content of that episode.
- I found no credible evidence on agent use inside Windows or Office, and the repositories above do not represent those proprietary codebases.
- The final 15,000 or so characters of Toub's post, which contain the concluding section, were not read.

## Amazon: what was built, how it is integrated, what the numbers mean, and what failed

### Takeaway
Amazon's Java upgrade claim is a modelled estimate, and its dollar figure comes partly from the Java 17 runtime rather than from the agent. The reported outages linked to agents remain in dispute between press sources and Amazon, and neither side has published a full incident record.

### Cited Findings

**Java upgrades on Amazon Q Developer**

- Amazon said it moved "tens of thousands" of production applications from Java 8 or 11 to Java 17, saving "over 4,500 years of development work for over a thousand developers" and yielding $260 million in annual savings. **[page]** — [AWS DevOps Blog, 1 August 2024](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/amazon-q-developer-just-reached-a-260-million-dollar-milestone)
- Method: Time saved was estimated by counting migrated Java dependencies at about a day or more each for a developer, against minutes for the agent. Cost savings were estimated by counting hosts removable because of Java 17 performance. AWS calls both estimates conservative. **[page]** — [AWS DevOps Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/amazon-q-developer-just-reached-a-260-million-dollar-milestone)
- Jassy cited more than 30,000 applications and an average upgrade falling from about 50 developer-days to a few hours, with $260 million described as annualised efficiency gains from better security and lower infrastructure costs. **[search]** — [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/amazons-ai-assistant-saves-4500-years-of-development-time-ceo-andy-jassy-says/); [Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/news/24/08/40537747/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-says-companys-ai-assistant-has-saved-260m-and-4-5k-developer-years-of-work-its)

**Kiro and Cost Explorer**

- The Financial Times, citing four sources through The Register, reported a 13-hour disruption after engineers allowed Kiro to make changes and the tool chose to "delete and recreate the environment". It reported that this was not the first time AI tools were involved in a service interruption. **[page]** — [The Register, 20 February 2026](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/amazon_denies_kiro_agentic_ai_behind_outage/)
- AWS said the event was "user (AWS employee) error - specifically misconfigured access controls - not AI", that it affected Cost Explorer in one of two mainland China regions, and that Kiro by default requests authorisation but the engineer's role had broader permissions than expected. **[page]** — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/amazon_denies_kiro_agentic_ai_behind_outage/)
- Amazon's own statement calls the FT story inaccurate, disputes a second event, says the problem could arise with any developer tool or manual action, reports no customer inquiries, and describes added safeguards including mandatory peer review for production access. **[page]** — [About Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/aws-service-outage-ai-bot-kiro)
- The FT reportedly linked a second disruption to Q Developer, and Amazon said that event did not affect a customer-facing service. This conflicts in tone with the About Amazon page, which disputes the second event. **[search]** — [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/computing/aws-suffered-at-least-two-outages-caused-by-ai-tools-and-now-im-convinced-were-living-inside-a-silicon-valley-episode)
- One report says Kiro was mandated as the standard internal assistant three weeks before the incident. This rests on one low-authority source that I did not corroborate. **[search]** — [Barrack AI](https://blog.barrack.ai/amazon-ai-agents-deleting-production/)

**Retail site incidents, March 2026**

- Per the FT as relayed by Fortune, an internal note cited a "trend of incidents" with "high blast radius" and "Gen-AI assisted changes", and Dave Treadwell wrote that site availability "has not been good recently". The FT reported that guardrails would include senior approval of AI-assisted changes by junior and mid-level engineers. Fortune did not verify this independently. **[page]** — [Fortune, 11 March 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/elon-musk-amazon-outage-ai-relate-incident-meeting-report-cybersecurity)
- Amazon said the meeting is a regular weekly review, AWS was not involved, only one incident discussed was AI-related and none involved AI-written code, and junior and mid-level engineers are not required to get senior sign-off. The last statement directly contradicts the reported email. **[page]** — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/elon-musk-amazon-outage-ai-relate-incident-meeting-report-cybersecurity)
- An outage earlier in March drew more than 22,000 Downdetector reports, and Amazon attributed it to "a software code deployment". **[page]** — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/elon-musk-amazon-outage-ai-relate-incident-meeting-report-cybersecurity)
- TechRadar's headline says even senior engineers need sign-off, which is an outlier against the other accounts. **[search]** — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/amazon-is-making-even-senior-engineers-get-code-signed-off-following-multiple-recent-outages)

### Inferences
- The $260 million figure largely measures the value of running Java 17, because it counts removed hosts, and any upgrade method would have delivered that value. Only the 4,500 years relates to agent speed, and that figure relies on an assumed per-dependency manual baseline rather than a measured comparison.
- Amazon's "not AI" framing and the FT framing can both be partly right, since excessive permissions enabled the failure while the agent's choice supplied the action. Agent permissions are thus a design property of the system, and calling this user error does not remove the design lesson.
- "None involved AI-written code" is narrower than "no AI involvement", since AI-assisted changes can include operations or configuration actions. The wording should be quoted exactly.

### Gaps
- I found no independent audit or investigative critique of the Java figures, and my search surfaced only restatements. The absence of scrutiny is itself a finding but should not be presented as confirmation.
- I did not read the FT originals, so all FT claims here are relayed.
- I found no public postmortem for either incident, and no public detail on Kiro or Q's internal context retrieval, sandboxing or merge-rate metrics.
- I recall a Jassy figure of 79% of auto-generated code reviews shipping without additional changes, but the AWS blog I read does not contain it, and I could not verify it elsewhere.

## Anthropic and OpenAI: how the vendors describe using their own agents

### Takeaway
Anthropic publishes unusually explicit self-caveats, including that its 8x figure overstates productivity and that Claude's code was worse than human code in late 2025, but nobody outside can check the data. OpenAI's harness engineering case is a greenfield product, so it is weak evidence for large existing codebases, and I could read it only through secondary sources.

### Cited Findings

**Anthropic**

- As of May 2026, "more than 80%" of code merged was authored by Claude, defined in a footnote as the share of lines merged to production that can be attributed to Claude. Anthropic calls the figure conservative because the attribution pipeline has gaps. Leadership's estimates of 90% or more include scripts and experimental code. **[page]** — [Anthropic Institute, "When AI builds itself", undated with an update on 18 September 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)
- The typical engineer merged 8x as much code per day as in 2024. The paper's introduction uses a different baseline and time unit, and it says the figure is "almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain". **[page]** — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)
- Anthropic says Claude-written code was "somewhat worse" than human code in late 2025 and is roughly at parity now. On the most open-ended tasks, success reached 76% in May 2026 as judged by a Claude judge. **[page]** — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)
- Workflow: Engineers direct and review while an automated Claude reviewer checks changes before merge. A retrospective found automated review of every change would have caught roughly a third of the bugs behind past claude.ai incidents. **[page]** — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)
- In a March 2026 poll of 130 research employees, the median respondent estimated 4x output. The authors expect that the true value was lower and cite METR's findings on overestimation. **[page]** — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)
- Study of August 2025 data, published 2 December 2025: The survey had 132 respondents, with 53 interviews and about 200,000 Claude Code transcripts. Self-reported productivity gain rose from 20% to 50%, and merged PRs per engineer per day rose 67%. More than half said they could fully delegate only 0 to 20% of their work. Some people spent more time on Claude-assisted tasks, mainly debugging. Skill atrophy and reduced mentorship were reported concerns. **[page]** — [Anthropic research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)
- That study names selection, social desirability and recall biases, and it notes responses were not anonymous and that findings may not generalise. **[page]** — [Anthropic research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)

**OpenAI**

- OpenAI's February 2026 post describes about 1,000,000 lines and about 1,500 merged PRs with zero manually written code over five months, with a team growing from three to seven engineers. The rate of 3.5 PRs per engineer per day is reported, and OpenAI estimates about one-tenth of the time of manual work. **[page, secondary]** — [Ry Walker profile](https://rywalker.com/research/openai-harness); primary, which returned 403: [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Architecture: AGENTS.md serves as a table of contents over a structured `docs/` directory, each isolated worktree has an ephemeral observability stack queried with LogQL and PromQL, Chrome DevTools Protocol is wired in for UI checks, and custom linters and structural tests enforce layering. Recurring "garbage collection" passes scan for drift. **[page, secondary]** — [Ry Walker profile](https://rywalker.com/research/openai-harness)
- Workflow: Most review is agent-to-agent, and humans may review but are not required to. **[page, secondary]** — [Ry Walker profile](https://rywalker.com/research/openai-harness)
- Limits noted by the profile: The project is a greenfield beta, line and PR counts are input metrics, no updated numbers have appeared since February 2026, and no failed approaches are documented. The profile's author runs Tembo, a commercial alternative in this market, which is a conflict of its own. A 500% increase in landed PRs belongs to Symphony, a separate orchestrator, and it should not be attributed to the harness work. **[page, secondary]** — [Ry Walker profile](https://rywalker.com/research/openai-harness)

### Inferences
- Both companies sell these agents, and model capability is central to their fundraising and marketing. Their numbers are best read as upper bounds on what a well-resourced, agent-native organisation with frontier model access can achieve.
- Anthropic's 80% is a line-attribution metric, and its 8x is volume. The company's own comments mean neither should be presented as a productivity multiplier.
- The OpenAI case falls near the edge of this report's scope, because it is greenfield. Its architecture patterns are useful, but its outcome numbers do not address legacy monorepos.

### Gaps
- I found no public incident or merge-rate dataset from either vendor comparable to dotnet/runtime.
- I found no primary description of how OpenAI uses Codex on its main production monorepo, and I could not read OpenAI's post directly.
- I did not read Anthropic's Claude Code best-practice documentation in this pass, which moved to [code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices), so context and sandbox details for its internal use are not covered here.
- Coverage claiming "low single digits" at the February 2025 launch of Claude Code is not confirmed on the primary page I read.

## Which claims are independently verifiable and which are vendor marketing?

### Takeaway
Only public PR records can be checked by outsiders, and among the evidence here this applies chiefly to the dotnet repositories. Everything else ranges from peer-reviewed self-report down to unaudited executive statements, and "share of code AI-generated" is not comparable between companies.

### Cited Findings

**Evidence tiers, from strongest to weakest**

- **Tier 1, recomputable by outsiders:** The dotnet/runtime and sibling repository statistics rest on public GitHub data, although the author is a Microsoft employee and Microsoft owns the product. — [.NET Blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/dotnet/ten-months-with-cca-in-dotnet-runtime/)
- **Tier 2, company-authored papers with denominators:** These cannot be reproduced externally, since the code and data are private. Examples are the [Google migration paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972), [Meta TestGen-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171) and [Meta JiT catching tests](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832). The Google paper's own authors disclaim comparative study status.
- **Tier 3, vendor self-study with stated caveats:** These describe internal data with disclosed bias, and the vendor sells the product. Examples are [Anthropic's December 2025 study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic) and [Anthropic's "When AI builds itself"](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement).
- **Tier 4, modelled or marketing numbers:** These include the [AWS 4,500 years and $260 million](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/amazon-q-developer-just-reached-a-260-million-dollar-milestone) and [Cursor's Nvidia story](https://cursor.com/blog/nvidia).
- **Tier 5, unaudited executive statements:** These include [Pichai's 75%](https://www.fastcompany.com/91531519/google-ceo-says-75-of-the-companys-code-is-ai-generated), [Nadella's 20 to 30%](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-microsoft-meta-code-written-by-ai/) and [Li's 30%](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4157175/meta-moves-fast-toward-a-world-where-ai-builds-the-software.html). Earnings call statements carry some legal exposure, but the definitions remain loose.
- **Contested press reporting:** The [Kiro incident](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/amazon_denies_kiro_agentic_ai_behind_outage/) and the [retail sign-off rule](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/elon-musk-amazon-outage-ai-relate-incident-meeting-report-cybersecurity) rest on anonymous sources and leaked email, and they face [direct denials from Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/aws-service-outage-ai-bot-kiro).

**Conflicts of interest**

- Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Anthropic and OpenAI all sell coding tools or the models behind them, and each benefits commercially from high adoption figures.
- Meta and Amazon reduced headcount in the same period, per [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4157175/meta-moves-fast-toward-a-world-where-ai-builds-the-software.html) and [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/11/elon-musk-amazon-outage-ai-relate-incident-meeting-report-cybersecurity), which gives them a reason to credit AI for productivity. This is my reading and the sources do not establish it as a motive.
- Critical sources also carry interests, since [the OpenAI profile's author runs a competing company](https://rywalker.com/research/openai-harness).

### Inferences
- Claims gain credibility when they state a denominator, a comparison group and a failure. The dotnet/runtime post does all three, Meta's JiT paper does most of them, and the executive percentages do none.
- A recurring pattern appears across otherwise unrelated sources: agents do best when a build and test loop exists, and human review becomes the constraint. Google, Microsoft and Anthropic each report the review constraint in their own material.
- Acceptance is not a measure of correctness. Reverts, incident rates and long-term maintenance cost are largely unreported, and [Google's paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972) states that long-term quality effects are unknown.

### Gaps
- I found no independent audit of any company's share-of-code figure.
- I found no study by outsiders that measures agent outcomes inside a proprietary big-tech monorepo.
- No company here publishes a time series of defect or incident rates attributed to agent-authored code.
