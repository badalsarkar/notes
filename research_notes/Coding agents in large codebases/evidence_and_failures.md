# Evidence and failures: AI coding productivity and quality in professional and large-codebase settings

Research date: 2026-11-02.

Verification key used in every table and list:

- **[P]** means that I read the primary paper, report or company statement during this session.
- **[A]** means that I read only the abstract, a landing page or a vendor marketing summary, so the methodology could not be checked.
- **[S]** means that the figure comes from a search excerpt or secondary coverage and was not checked against the original. Treat it as a lead. The search tool returned a summary and a list of result URLs without tying each claim to one URL, so the link on an [S] claim is my best match from that list and must be opened before the claim is quoted.

## Controlled and quasi-experimental studies

### Takeaway
The only clean randomised evidence for agents on mature code is now about 18 months old or admitted by its authors to be unreliable, so no current, credible, randomised effect size for agents in large codebases exists. Quasi-experiments show a consistent pattern in which velocity rises and static-analysis complexity rises persistently, but none of them measures delivered value, and the most positive agent result comes from a vendor's own employees and does not measure quality.

### Inferences
- The strongest statement the data supports is about direction rather than magnitude. Agents probably increase merged output in early 2026, but the size is unknown and the quality cost is unmeasured in the only large enterprise study that exists.
- METR's follow-up should not be read as either "AI still slows developers" or "the slowdown has reversed". Its point estimates include zero, and METR itself says that the design has failed. Developers refusing to work without AI is behavioural evidence of perceived value, and it is not evidence of productivity.
- Agarwal et al. qualify He et al.'s claim that velocity gains vanish. Velocity persisted for agent-first repositories and faded or reversed for repositories with prior IDE AI use. A sceptical reading is that the IDE-first group is closer to mature professional projects, while the agent-first sample is selected on having at least 10 agent PRs. A less sceptical reading is that agents differ from IDE assistants.
- Every quasi-experiment that measured static-analysis complexity found a persistent rise. This is the most replicated quality signal, although the replications share authors, an estimator and SonarQube, and they therefore share failure modes.

### Gaps
- I found no 2026 randomised controlled trial of agents with a no-AI control arm in an enterprise large-codebase setting. METR lists [fixed-task and shorter designs](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) as options, but I found no results from them.
- I did not find a Stanford paper with measured results by codebase size. See the next-but-one section for why the widely repeated curve should not be cited as a measurement.
- I did not retrieve Google's exact confidence interval or the Cui per-site estimates.

### Inferences
- Faros and DX directly conflict. Faros reports incidents per PR up about 3x when comparing high and low adopters, while DX reports flat change failure rates over time. The two differ in denominator (incidents per PR versus failure rate on deployments), in comparison (cross-sectional versus time series), and in the commercial product each sells. Neither should be cited alone.
- Faros's incidents per PR is a difficult metric because incident counts depend on reporting practices and PR size changes. Its monthly incidents figure of +57.9% is less dramatic and is the better one to quote.
- All organisation-level adoption comparisons are vulnerable to GitClear's finding that the highest-output developers were already the highest-output before AI.
- A vendor that sells measurement has an interest in the claim that measurement is missing, so agreement among these vendors is weaker than the number of reports suggests.

### Gaps
- After DORA's withdrawal, no independent annual survey with a stable methodology appears to exist.
- I could not read the gated Faros reports, and the primary CircleCI PDF was not read in full.
- I did not retrieve Stack Overflow's own results pages.

## Why individual speed-up fails to reach organisational outcomes, and how results vary by context

### Takeaway
The data most consistently points to a bottleneck moving from writing code to review, integration and CI, combined with larger PRs and rework. The evidence on codebase size is much weaker than commonly stated, and the evidence on seniority actively conflicts.

### Cited Findings

#### Mechanisms
- **Larger changes.** DX reports [median PR size up 122% year on year](https://getdx.com/news/new-dx-data-finds-94-of-ai-token-usage-is-re-reading-existing-context/), and Faros reports [+51% at high adoption](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash). VENDOR.
- **Review queues.** LinearB reports that [AI PRs wait 4.6x longer for pickup, then get reviewed 2x faster](https://linearb.io/resources/engineering-benchmarks-report), which suggests that reviewers avoid them and then give them less attention. That suggestion is my interpretation. VENDOR.
- **Integration failures.** CircleCI shows [feature-branch activity growing while median main-branch throughput stays flat or falls](https://circleci.com/blog/five-takeaways-2026-q2-pulse/). VENDOR.
- **Downstream delivery.** Faros reports [epics up 66.2% while deployments per week fell 11.7%](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash). VENDOR.
- **Rework.** The Stanford talk says [apparent output of +30% to +40% falls to +15% to +20% after rework](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/tbDDYKRFjhk-ai-developer-productivity).
- **Compounding complexity.** He et al. estimate that [higher complexity and warnings reduce later velocity](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v3), so that a roughly 3x rise in complexity or a 5x rise in warnings would offset the Cursor velocity gain.
- **Perception.** METR found a [gap of over 40 percentage points](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/) between belief and measurement, and the Stanford talk found [self-assessment off by about 30 percentile points among 43 developers](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/tbDDYKRFjhk-ai-developer-productivity). Organisations that rely on self-reported savings may therefore not be losing a gain; the gain may be smaller than reported.
- **Weak delivery perception.** DX reports [perceived ease of delivery up only 3%](https://getdx.com/news/new-dx-data-finds-94-of-ai-token-usage-is-re-reading-existing-context/) despite the throughput growth. VENDOR.
- **Amplification.** DORA 2025 describes [AI as an amplifier of existing strengths and dysfunctions](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report?e=48754805). This is an interpretive frame that the data does not test causally.

#### Larger versus smaller codebases, and legacy versus new
- **Stanford matrix (indicative).** [Greenfield low complexity +30% to +40%, greenfield high complexity +10% to +15%, brownfield low complexity +15% to +20%, and brownfield high complexity 0% to +10%, with some work slower](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/tbDDYKRFjhk-ai-developer-productivity). The sample behind this is 136 teams at 27 companies, not 100,000 developers.
- **Stanford codebase-size curve.** The [written summary of the talk presents the 1,000-to-10-million-line curve as an illustrative hypothesis without numeric values](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/tbDDYKRFjhk-ai-developer-productivity). Secondary articles that report it as a measured result overstate it. I only read the summary, so the talk video should be checked before relying on this reading.
- **Agent benchmark.** RepoMod-Bench reports [pass rates of about 15% for projects over 50,000 lines](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.22518), although this concerns repository translation rather than ordinary engineering. [S]
- **Codebase quality.** The Stanford December 2025 talk reports [an R² of about 0.40 between gains and codebase cleanliness, compared with a correlation of about 0.20 with token use](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/JvosMkuNxF8-quantify-ai-roi-in-software-engineering-stanford). [S]
- **Mature projects.** METR's 2025 RCT [slowed experienced developers on mature projects](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089), and Agarwal et al. [speculate that diminishing returns in IDE-first repositories reflect coordination and review costs in mature projects](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13597).
- **Size controls.** He et al. [found that codebase growth explains much of the variance in warnings and complexity but did not stratify by size](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v3). Microsoft [did not analyse repository size](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01418).

#### Language popularity
- The Stanford summary reports [about +20% on simpler work and +10% to +15% on complex work in widely used languages, and less reliable assistance with possible slowdowns on complex tasks in COBOL, Haskell and Elixir, without numbers for those languages](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/tbDDYKRFjhk-ai-developer-productivity). This is the only source that I found, and I rate it as low confidence.

| Earlier finding | Newer finding | Status |
|---|---|---|

### Inferences
- The most defensible explanation is a shift in the constraint from authoring to verification and integration, which is consistent with queueing behaviour. This is an inference from correlational vendor data, as no study has tested the mechanism causally at the organisation level.
- The size of the "large codebase penalty" is not established. Task complexity, brownfield status, codebase cleanliness and project maturity are confounded, and they may matter more than line count. A report should say that gains appear lower on complex brownfield work, and it should not claim a measured curve by lines of code.
- The seniority conflict may reflect tool era and outcome. Completion tools may help juniors complete tasks, while agents may reward people who can specify and review work. This is plausible but untested.

### Gaps
- I found no study that isolates repository size within an enterprise monorepo while also measuring human productivity.
- I found no causal evidence about organisational outcomes such as revenue, cycle time for features or customer value.
- I found no direct measurements for legacy languages beyond the Stanford summary.

## Benchmarks for large-repository capability

### Takeaway
Passing automated tests substantially overstates mergeable work, and the headline benchmarks have themselves been shown to be contaminated or poorly graded. Benchmark scores should therefore be treated as upper bounds that are not comparable across scaffolds.

### Inferences
- The METR note and the Datacurve audit err in opposite directions. METR suggests that passing overstates quality, while Datacurve reports that 24% of correct solutions are wrongly rejected. The net bias in a given score is therefore unknown, and it is not safe to assume that real capability is simply lower.
- The three context-file studies are not necessarily contradictory because they measure success, efficiency, and long-term code quality respectively. A report should not claim that AGENTS.md is proven either to help or to hurt.

### Gaps
- The CAIS page on SWE-bench Pro [returned HTTP 404](https://safe.ai/blog/evaluating-ai-progress-on-swe-bench-pro), so its content is missing.
- I did not view the current Scale leaderboard directly, and scores are therefore not current.
- I found no public benchmark that tests agents on repositories of enterprise monorepo scale, such as tens of millions of lines. The retrieval-focused literature is covered more thoroughly by [existing notes in this repo](../Knowledge%20graphs%20for%20coding%20agents/codebase_knowledge_at_scale.md).

## Documented incidents and failures

### Takeaway
Incidents are real but they are anecdotes with no denominator, so they establish failure modes and not rates. The recurring root cause is excessive credentials and blast radius rather than coding ability alone. Cost overruns are the best-documented enterprise failure in 2026, while rollbacks of coding mandates are less documented than headlines imply.

#### Junior pipeline and skill atrophy
- **Skill formation.** The [Anthropic RCT](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) found scores of 50% vs 67% (17 percentage points, d = 0.738, p = 0.01) with the largest gap on debugging, and with no significant speed gain. Usage patterns mattered: conceptual questioning scored at 65% or above, and delegation scored below 40%, with very small subgroups. Long-term retention was not measured.
- **Employment.** [Brynjolfsson et al.'s revision of 12 August 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recen) puts employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, using ADP payroll data through June 2026. The authors describe this as descriptive rather than causal.
- **Software-specific figure.** The [commonly cited decline of about 20% for developers aged 22 to 25 from the late 2022 peak](https://bigthink.com/business/are-young-workers-canaries-in-the-ai-coal-mine/) comes from the earlier version and is [frequently misdated or repeated as if independently confirmed](https://dev.to/thesythesis/the-inexperience-tax-ne). [S]
- **Counter-signal.** [NACE reportedly revised Class of 2026 hiring projections up to +5.6%, while young graduate unemployment reportedly reached 5.3%](https://www.softwareseni.com/what-the-data-actually-shows-about-ai-and-junior-developer-employment-decline/). [S] The timing of the decline also overlaps with the post-2022 interest-rate cycle, which is a plausible alternative that I did not test.

### Inferences
- PocketOS, Kiro (under either account) and Replit share a pattern in which an agent or an operator's tool had authority over production that was not scoped to the task. Controls on credentials and destructive operations are therefore a stronger lesson than any claim about model quality.
- Amazon's two statements need careful handling. In February it [listed mandatory peer review for production access as a new safeguard](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/aws-service-outage-ai-bot-kiro), and in March it [denied new approval requirements for engineers working with AI tools](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-outage-ai-financial-times-correction). They are not strictly contradictory, but they should not be flattened into "Amazon requires senior sign-off on AI code", which it denies.
- Reports of AI layoffs being reversed in engineering are mostly unsourced trend articles. A sceptical review should apply the same standard to these as to vendor productivity claims.
- Cost overruns may be a sign of high demand rather than failure. Uber's overrun shows that budgets were wrong, and it does not show that the spending lacked value, although the COO's remark suggests that value is hard to demonstrate.

### Gaps
- I found no incident rate or denominator for agent-caused outages, and no independent public post-mortem for Kiro or the March Amazon events.
- I searched for a "6.3 million orders" figure for the March Amazon event, which I recalled without a source, and found nothing that supports it, so it should not be cited from these notes.
- I did not read The Information or the FT originals; the Register and TechSpot pieces on PocketOS were also blocked (HTTP 403), so those details are from excerpts.
- I found no longitudinal study of skill atrophy among professional engineers.
- I found no well-sourced, named, enterprise-wide AI coding pilot that was formally abandoned for lack of results. This absence may reflect non-disclosure rather than non-occurrence.
