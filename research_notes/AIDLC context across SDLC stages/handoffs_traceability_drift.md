# Handoffs, Artefact Identity, Traceability, Drift and Audit Across SDLC Stages

These notes are current to 1 November 2026. Evidence labels are: **[Peer]** for peer-reviewed work, **[Preprint]** for unreviewed work, **[Vendor]** for a claim by the party that builds or sells the item, **[Secondary]** for commentary, **[Primary law]** for regulatory text that was read directly, and **[Older]** for material from before 2026. Where a label says "snippet", the claim came from a search summary and the page itself was not read.

## How much information is lost across stage or agent handoffs, and which contract patterns work?

### Takeaway
No source found measures information loss per handoff directly. The evidence consists of failure taxonomies and system comparisons, and these support structured artefacts and independent verification more strongly than they support any claim about a percentage lost.

### Cited Findings
- MAST annotated more than 1,600 traces across seven multi-agent frameworks and identified 14 failure modes in three categories. Human inter-annotator agreement was κ = 0.88, and an LLM annotator reached κ = 0.77 against human experts. [Preprint, v3 of 26 October 2025; Older] — [Cemri et al., abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657); [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)
- MAST reports these shares of failures: reasoning-action mismatch 13.2%, task derailment 7.4%, failure to ask for clarification 6.8%, conversation reset 2.2%, ignored another agent's input 1.9% and information withholding 0.85%. In the system design category, loss of conversation history is 2.8%, disobeying the task specification 11.8% and step repetition 15.7%. In the verification category, the shares are incorrect verification 9.1%, no or incomplete verification 8.2% and premature termination 6.2%. — [MAST full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)
- Summing those modes gives about 44.2% for system design, 32.4% for inter-agent misalignment and 23.5% for verification. A search summary gives 32.3% for misalignment, and secondary sources also circulate figures of 31% and about 37%, so those two figures should not be cited. — [MAST full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3); [alphaXiv overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2503.13657v3)
- MAST reports failure rates of 41% to 86.7% across seven open-source systems, but notes that the benchmarks differ and the rates are not directly comparable. — [MAST full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)
- MAST states that many verifiers run only superficial checks, such as whether code compiles. Adding a high-level objective check to ChatDev raised task success on ProgramDev by 15.6%. — [MAST full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)
- MetaGPT has agents exchange role-specific documents and diagrams rather than dialogue, using a shared message pool with publish-subscribe filtering. An agent acts only after receiving its prerequisite inputs. [Older, 2023; authors' own evaluation] — [Hong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.00352)
- In the MetaGPT authors' comparison with ChatDev, executability was 3.75 against 2.25 on a 1 to 4 scale, human revision cost was 0.83 against 2.5, and token use was 31,255 against 19,292. This compares whole systems and does not isolate the effect of the schema. — [Hong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.00352)
- Cognition's first principle is to "Share context, and share full agent traces, not just individual messages". [Vendor, 12 June 2025; Older] — [Yan, Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)
- A 2026 Cognition follow-up is reported as concluding that multi-agent work is most effective when writing remains single-threaded and other agents contribute analysis, and that a reviewer works best from a clean context rather than the coder's reasoning. The original was not read and secondary dates conflict. [Secondary, snippet] — [Latent Space wiki](https://latent.space/wiki/walden-yan-7c572c23/); [traeai summary](https://www.traeai.com/articles/f89070d0-6418-4e01-b0ae-d02b3fbb81f4)
- Anthropic briefs each subagent with an objective, output format, tool guidance and task boundaries. It also has subagents store work in external systems and pass lightweight references back, which it says reduces the "game of telephone". It reports that multi-agent systems use about 15 times the tokens of chat. [Vendor, 13 June 2025; Older] — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
- "Decomposition Buys Integrity, Not Yield" reports that handoff count alone accounts for failures in ChatDev and Magentic. Only a search snippet was read, because the paper did not load. [Preprint, snippet, unverified] — [arXiv 2609.17464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17464)
- AgentAsk argues that multi-agent systems need agents that ask clarifying questions at handoffs. Only the title and listing were seen. [Preprint, snippet] — [arXiv 2510.07593](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07593)
- Pattern evidence in shipping tools: AWS AI-DLC "stops at approval gates" and advertises "source-bound review evidence" [Vendor] — [aidlc-workflows](https://github.com/awslabs/aidlc-workflows). BMAD runs each story in a fresh chat with the story file as the handoff package, and the user approves the story before development [Secondary, third-party mirrors] — [BMAD user guide mirror](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/src/tag/v5.1.0/docs/user-guide.md).

### Inferences
- The Cognition and Anthropic positions are less opposed than they are often presented. Both reject a lossy summary as the carrier of context. Cognition's answer is to share the full trace with a writer, while Anthropic's is to pass a reference to a durable artefact. A delivery platform can use both: artefact references between stages, and a clean context for independent review.
- MAST's small shares for explicit handoff modes do not show that handoffs are safe. Its authors note that withholding can resemble other failures, and a downstream agent that silently acts on an incomplete artefact may be labelled as derailment or reasoning-action mismatch.
- Typed schemas address missing fields but not wrong content. The verification shares in MAST suggest that a gate must check meaning as well as form. For a restriction engine, a schema can require a precedence rule to be present, but only a test or a reviewer can establish that the rule is correct.
- Reviewer independence also serves audit. A reviewer agent with a clean context is closer to the segregation of functions expected in change management, although no regulator text found says this about agents.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study that measures information retained per handoff, or that compares typed schemas, handoff documents, blackboards and gates under the same conditions.
- MetaGPT's blackboard result is not separated from its role and workflow design. I found no independent replication.

## How should artefacts be identified and versioned, and what do current tools do?

### Takeaway
The tools found identify artefacts mainly by file path, feature number or naming convention, and none of the documentation read specifies per-requirement content hashes, supersession records or event sourcing. A regulated platform would therefore have to add these itself.

### Cited Findings
- Spec Kit assigns sequential feature numbers (001, 002 and so on), creates a semantic branch and uses a `specs/[branch-name]/` directory holding `spec.md`, `plan.md` and `tasks.md`, with `memory/constitution.md` for project principles. [Vendor] — [spec-driven.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/spec-kit/main/spec-driven.md)
- OpenSpec separates live specs in `openspec/specs/` from change folders in `openspec/changes/<change-name>/`. Finished changes move to `openspec/changes/archive/<date>-<change-name>/`, and the archive step updates the specs. Requirements appear under `## ADDED Requirements` with WHEN/THEN scenarios. [Vendor] — [OpenSpec README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec/main/README.md)
- Kiro generates `requirements.md` (or `bugfix.md`), `design.md` and `tasks.md`. Its documentation says EARS notation makes requirements traceable, but the page read does not explain how a requirement maps to a task. [Vendor] — [Kiro specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/); [Kiro feature specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/feature-specs/)
- BMAD shards large documents by level-2 heading with an index file and names stories `{epicNum}.{storyNum}.{storyTitle}.md`. No requirement-to-story matrix was found in the material read. [Secondary, third-party mirrors] — [shard-doc mirror](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/src/commit/0881735a20efd92dfbd619a739ad1484761a9ac2/bmad-core/tasks/shard-doc.md); [BMAD user guide mirror](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/src/tag/v5.1.0/docs/user-guide.md)
- Tessl links a spec to code with `@generate` for generated files and `@describe` for existing code, and uses `@test` for test links. Its full framework is described as a closed beta. [Vendor] — [Tessl spec syntax](https://docs.tessl.io/reference/spec-syntax)
- AWS AI-DLC's README, at badge version 2.11.2, describes a "131-event audit trail", persistent state, 5 phases, 33 stages, 14 agents and 11 workflow profiles. The README does not define an identifier scheme or storage format. [Vendor] — [aidlc-workflows](https://github.com/awslabs/aidlc-workflows)
- ReqIF 1.2 is the formal OMG interchange format, dated July 2016. [Older] — [OMG ReqIF](https://www.omg.org/spec/ReqIF)
- Agent Trace has an optional per-range `content_hash`, with `murmur3:9f2e8a1b` as its example, for tracking attributed code that moves. [RFC, January 2026] — [Agent Trace](https://agent-trace.dev/)

### Inferences
- A workable model separates a stable logical identifier from a version digest, for example `REQ-RESTRICT-014` plus a SHA-256 of the canonicalised content. Links should record the upstream digest they were derived from, because staleness can then be computed by comparison. This is a design proposal and no tool found implements it.
- Paths and sequence numbers are weak identifiers for audit. A renamed file, a branch-scoped folder or an archived change can break a reference.
- OpenSpec's separation of live specs from dated change records is the closest found to a supersession model, since the archive retains why each change happened. It does not by itself record which code or approval depended on which version.
- A hybrid is the most defensible storage model: Git as the reviewed source for authored artefacts, and an append-only event store for gates, approvals and link events, from which a queryable graph can be rebuilt. Git history can be rewritten and is awkward to query, while a database alone loses the pull request review path. Murmur3 is suitable for relocating code but not as tamper evidence, because it is not a cryptographic hash.

### Gaps
- I found no primary evidence comparing Git, database and event-sourced artefact stores for this use.
- Spec Kit's per-requirement identifiers, the Kiro task-to-requirement linkage and the internal format of AI-DLC's audit events were not confirmed from the pages read. The AI-DLC 2.0 specification PDF and developer reference were not read.
- The official BMAD repository was not read, so BMAD details may differ by version.

## What is the evidence for traceability recovery, and which standards and tools are relevant?

### Takeaway
LLM link recovery is suited to proposing candidates for human review, and the available figures do not support using it as an audit record. Recording links when work happens is therefore preferable to recovering them later, and the provenance standards found cover code attribution or build integrity rather than the full chain from requirement to approval.

### Cited Findings
- TraceLLM evaluated eight LLMs on four benchmark datasets. On CM1 its best configuration reached precision 0.45, recall 0.67 and F2 0.61, and zero-shot prompting reached 0.52, 0.59 and 0.57. Figures for the other datasets were not read. Its abstract supports semi-automated workflows in which analysts validate candidate links. [Peer per listed journal reference, Requirements Engineering 2026] — [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01253); [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01253)
- An LLM ensemble reached F2 comparable to information retrieval methods with lower precision, across five projects. Small LLMs reduced candidates with little recall loss, and information retrieval results depended heavily on project-specific tuning. Exact figures were not on the page read. [Peer workshop, 2025; Older] — [Fuchß et al.](https://fuchss.org/conferences/aire25/)
- LiSSA's documentation shows a requirements-to-code sample on what appears to be Dronology: 283 true positives, 1,286 false positives and 457 false negatives, or precision of about 0.18 and recall of about 0.38. This is a sample output and must not be cited as LiSSA's benchmark result. — [LiSSA](https://helmholtz.software/software/lissa)
- A 2025 KIT thesis reports that an agentic requirements-to-code approach fell short of traditional information retrieval on both precision and recall. [Thesis, snippet; Older] — [KIT](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000183058)
- Agent Trace 0.1.0 is an RFC from January 2026. It records a VCS revision, files, conversations and line ranges, a `provider/model-name` model identifier, an optional conversation URL, and contributor types `human`, `ai`, `mixed` and `unknown`. It does not define storage, and code ownership, training data provenance and quality assessment are non-goals. Its schema's version pattern would reject the three-part version in its own examples. — [Agent Trace](https://agent-trace.dev/)
- git-ai has agents report their edits through `git-ai checkpoint`, then stores attribution in Git Notes per commit, linking lines to agent, model and session. It claims to carry attribution through rebase, squash and cherry-pick, but says renames through `git mv` and `filter-repo` are not tracked. Server-side squash merges need its Teams product or CI actions. [Vendor, unverified] — [git-ai](https://github.com/git-ai-project/git-ai)
- Entire CLI is described as capturing prompts, tool calls and diffs as checkpoints on an orphan branch, launched in February 2026. A comparison page frames Entire as recording the conversation and git-ai as recording which lines are AI-written. [Secondary] — [AI traceability guide](https://cc.bruniaux.com/guide/ai-traceability/); [con/serve](https://con.github.io/serve/tools/ai-sessions/git-ai)
- The in-toto attestation repository defines verifiable claims about how software is produced, plus vetted predicates. A 2026 conference abstract states that AI models remain outside established provenance frameworks such as SLSA and in-toto. I found no predicate for agent-generated code. — [in-toto attestation fork](https://github.com/adityasaky/in-toto-attestation); [OpenSSF abstract](https://openssfcdeu2026.sched.com/event/2RA4B)
- The SPDX 3 AI profile describes AI packages: model type, domain, standards compliance and how the model is used in the application. It describes a component and does not record one generated change. — [SPDX 3 AI model](https://github.com/spdx/spdx-3-model/blob/main/model/AI/AI.md)
- OSLC Requirements Management 2.1 was approved as an OASIS Project Specification in 2020, and its later status could not be confirmed. [Older] — [OSLC specifications](https://www.open-services.net/specifications)
- Jama ships an official MCP server with Jama Connect 9.35 as a separately purchased add-on, and says existing permissions, lifecycle workflows and audit trails apply. A third-party page says it has 18 tools and excludes bulk operations. [Vendor; Secondary for the tool count] — [Jama 9.35 release notes](https://support.jamasoftware.com/hc/en-us/articles/45837542601229-Version-9-35-Customer-Validated-Cloud); [third-party listing](https://repos.skila.ai/servers/jama-connect-mcp-server)
- An open-source package, `mcp-server-polarion`, reads and writes Polarion documents and work items, including traceability links. It is not established here as a Siemens product. — [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/mcp-server-polarion/0.2.0/)
- Commit conventions: LLVM asks contributors to disclose substantial tool-generated content, using a trailer such as `Assisted-by`, and holds the contributor fully accountable. The Linux kernel page read says AI agents "MUST NOT add Signed-off-by tags" because only humans can certify the DCO, and it did not define an `Assisted-by` format. Claude Code's default of `Co-Authored-By` is described in a secondary guide. — [LLVM policy](https://llvm.org/docs/AIToolPolicy.html); [Linux kernel](https://docs.kernel.org/process/coding-assistants.html); [AI traceability guide](https://cc.bruniaux.com/guide/ai-traceability/)

### Inferences
- At a precision near 0.5, about one proposed link in two would be wrong on the TraceLLM CM1 result. Recovered links in an audit record should therefore carry a distinct status, such as `proposed`, until a named person confirms them.
- A complete chain needs several layers: Git and work-item identifiers for requirement links, Agent Trace or git-ai for line attribution, a signed attestation for the approval and build, and a platform record for the context supplied. No single standard found covers them all.
- An in-toto custom predicate is a plausible carrier for a signed record of model, context manifest digest, artefact digests and approver, but this would be an in-house convention.
- Trailers suit disclosure but are weak evidence, because they are self-asserted and a squash merge can alter them. They can be used as an index into stronger records.

### Gaps
- I found no official DOORS Next or Polarion MCP server. A May 2026 claim that neither was live could not be verified, and IBM's OSLC documentation was not read. The conventional route to DOORS is its OSLC or REST interface, which was not confirmed here.
- I found no independent evaluation of the attribution accuracy of Agent Trace, git-ai or Entire.
- The SLSA specification and the in-toto main repository were not read directly. The repository cited above is a fork.

## How is spec-to-code drift detected and managed, and how often do specs go stale?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on how often specifications go stale was not found. Automated drift detection in current tools appears limited, and the most relevant new evidence is that coding agents tend to follow a stale instruction file over the code even after they notice the conflict.

### Cited Findings
- Böckeler defines three levels. Spec-first writes a spec for the task at hand, spec-anchored keeps it for the feature's evolution, and spec-as-source makes it the main edited file. She classes Kiro and Spec Kit as spec-first, and says Tessl aspires to spec-anchored and is exploring spec-as-source. [Independent practitioner, 15 October 2025; Older, so the tools may have moved] — [Böckeler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)
- Xu and Wu built 40 Python projects in which an instruction file conflicted with the code, and ran Claude Code and Codex CLI for 320 runs. Without intervention, 90.1% of choices followed the stale file. Claude Code flagged the conflict in 79 of 80 runs but still followed the file. Codex rewrote existing code in 20 of 80 runs, and tests failed in 17 of those. One sentence giving existing code precedence raised code-following choices from 4.2% to 91.4% and test pass rates from 89.4% to 100%. [Preprint, 22 October 2026; synthetic conflicts about coding conventions] — [arXiv 2610.30571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30571)
- The Spec Growth Engine proposes a drift gate that makes spec-code divergence "a blocking merge condition", and a companion paper describes a deterministic engine comparing the spec graph with the code's import graph. The abstracts read report no outcome figures, and the companion's tables were not read. [Preprints, single author, 2026] — [arXiv 2606.27045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27045); [arXiv 2610.11725](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11725)
- Spec Kit claims that specifications "stay in sync with code because they generate it", that a changed PRD flags or updates affected plans, and that consistency checks run continuously. The page does not explain how hand-edited code or conflicts are handled. [Vendor claim; compare Böckeler's spec-first classification] — [spec-driven.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/spec-kit/main/spec-driven.md)
- Kiro's Analyze Requirements checks requirements for inconsistency and gaps before design. Third-party guides describe Refine on `design.md` and Sync Files on `tasks.md` for propagation, and warn that direct edits to `tasks.md` can leave the spec out of date. [Vendor for Analyze; Secondary for the rest] — [Kiro feature specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/feature-specs/); [Verdent guide](https://www.verdent.ai/guides/agents/kiro-spec-driven-development)
- The Spec Growth Engine paper is reported to say that Kiro's specs are deleted after a feature ships. No Kiro documentation was found to confirm this. [Secondary, snippet, unconfirmed] — [arXiv 2606.27045](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27045)
- Tessl's published spec-verification skill compares target files with spec requirements, flags code paths that no requirement covers, and instructs that the spec be updated first and then re-verified. Its product documentation shows no separate drift-detection feature in the pages seen. [Vendor] — [Tessl skill](https://tessl.io/registry/tessl-labs/spec-driven-development/files/skills/spec-verification/SKILL.md); [Tessl spec syntax](https://docs.tessl.io/reference/spec-syntax)
- Adjacent evidence on documentation decay: a study of code-comment inconsistency reports that the effect on bug introduction is strongest soon after the inconsistency appears and fades over time. This concerns comments and does not measure specifications. [Preprint, 2024; Older; snippet] — [Radmanesh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10781v1)
- Tan, Wagner and Treude studied outdated code element references in repository documentation, including how long they remain outdated and how projects resolve them. Only a summary page was seen and no rate was extracted. [Peer, 2024; Older] — [summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/detecting-outdated-code-element-references-in-software-repository-documentation/934656791608819769-2499)

### Inferences
- Drift detection works at three levels that catch different faults. Reference checks find removed symbols, structural checks compare a declared graph with the import graph, and behavioural checks run tests derived from requirements. Only the last would catch a precedence rule whose code still compiles but behaves differently.
- Impact analysis follows from digests on links. When an upstream version changes, each artefact derived from the earlier digest can be marked `suspect` and its approval marked as no longer current. This is the established "suspect link" idea from requirements management tools, although its use in the tools covered here was not confirmed.
- Full bidirectional synchronisation appears unsafe in a regulated setting, because automatically editing a spec to match code could legitimise a defect. A safer rule is that code-to-spec differences open a proposed change requiring approval, as Tessl's "update the spec first" guidance partly implies.
- Xu and Wu imply that detection alone is not enough. Their results make the authority order an explicit design decision. For a stage instruction the code may be the better evidence, while for a client mandate rule the approved requirement should prevail and the conflict should stop the work for human decision.
- Spec-as-source repeats the model-driven development trade-off and depends on regeneration being deterministic enough to review. This is hard to defend for a production trading change, so spec-anchored with drift gates is the more practical default.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring how often specifications produced by spec-driven tools become stale, and none measuring the same for instruction files in real repositories. Xu and Wu measure the consequence of staleness and not its frequency.
- I found no independent evaluation of the precision and recall of any drift detector in these tools.
- OpenSpec's strict validation command was seen only in a secondary source, and whether it checks code was not confirmed. — [SDD tools overview](https://jmlopezdona.github.io/ai-coding-agents-sdd/07-native-sdd-tools/)

## What do regulators require for AI-generated change: who approved what, on what evidence, and with which model and context?

### Takeaway
None of the texts confirmed here sets a rule specific to AI-generated code. Existing change rules already require evidence of who changed and who approved, while the requirement to record model and context is a reasoned control design and not something quoted from a regulator.

### Cited Findings
- **SR 26-2, confirmed from primary text.** Footnote 3 states: "Generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within the scope of this guidance." It adds that risk management and governance practices should guide controls for tools not covered. The letter is expected to be most relevant to organisations above $30 billion in assets and supersedes SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. [Primary law, supervisory guidance] — [Federal Reserve SR 26-2](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.htm); [attachment](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602a1.pdf)
- The OCC issued the same guidance as Bulletin 2026-13 on 17 April 2026 and says the agencies plan a request for information covering banks' use of AI, including generative and agentic AI. [Primary law] — [OCC Bulletin 2026-13](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-13.html)
- **MiFID II RTS 6 Article 5, read in the UK legislation copy of Regulation (EU) 2017/589.** Firms must set methodologies for developing and testing algorithms before deployment or substantial update, with further testing after substantial changes. They must keep records of material changes to algorithmic trading software that allow determination of when a change was made, who made it, who approved it and its nature. Senior management must designate a person to authorise deployment or substantial update. [Primary law through a UK copy; the EUR-Lex original was not accessible] — [Article 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/589/article/5)
- RTS 6 Article 9 requires an annual self-assessment and validation, with a risk management report audited by internal audit where that function exists and approved by senior management. The UK page marks this provision as revoked for the UK under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023, so UK applicability needs separate checking. — [Article 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/589/article/9)
- RTS 6 Article 28(3) sets a five-year period for the high-frequency trading order records in paragraphs 1 and 2. A secondary summary that cites Article 28 as a general algorithm record rule was therefore not supported by the text as read. — [Article 28, as adopted](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/589/article/28/adopted); [secondary summary](https://cdn.presencis.com/regulations/mifid2/article-record-keeping/)
- MiFID II Delegated Regulation 2017/565 Article 72(1) requires records in a medium accessible to the authority, with corrections identifiable and earlier contents retained, no other manipulation possible, and IT-based analysis possible where manual review is impractical. It concerns records under MiFID generally, so its application to software change records is an interpretation. The retention period was not on the page read. [Primary law through the UK copy as adopted] — [Article 72, as adopted](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/565/article/72/adopted)
- **DORA RTS 2024/1774 Article 17, NOT confirmed from primary text.** Secondary mirrors and fragments indicate that it requires independence of the functions approving changes from those requesting and implementing them, clear roles, documentation of change details, fall-back procedures, assessment of the effect on security measures, and post-implementation documentation and approval of emergency changes. — [Springlex mirror](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dora/rts-rmf-regulation/article-17/); [Advisera](https://advisera.com/cdr-2024-1774/ict-change-management/)
- **SOX, NOT confirmed from primary text.** PCAOB's AS 2201 page refused the request, and only secondary material was available. It describes program change as an IT general control domain, with evidence usually drawn from tickets and source control, and an emergency path with after-the-fact review. I found nothing specific to AI-generated code. — [Safeguard, vendor](https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/sox-itgc-policies-and-documents-you-need); [Federal Register, AS 1105 amendments, not read in full](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-07-02/html/2024-14488.htm)
- Practitioner reports note that the FCA's review of algorithmic trading controls found weak documentation of development and testing procedures as an area of concern. [Secondary, snippet] — [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/fca-multi-firm-review-of-algorithmic-trading-controls/)

### Inferences
- SR 26-2's exclusion is commonly misread. It removes generative and agentic models from this guidance but points firms to their general governance practices. It also addresses model risk in a bank's models and does not address a coding tool's change control, so it is not the main control text for AI-assisted delivery.
- RTS 6 Article 5 gives a concrete minimum for an algorithmic trading change: time, author, approver and nature. A platform that captures only Git author and merge approver meets the literal fields only if an agent's work is attributed clearly and a named person is accountable for it. Treating the human submitter as author, as LLVM and the Linux kernel do, fits this, while the agent should appear as a tool.
- A proposed minimum audit record for each change is: artefact identifiers and digests, the requesting and approving persons, the approver's role and independence, evidence reviewed at the gate, model identifier and version, prompt and context manifest digest, tool actions, test results, deployment and rollback references, and whether the approval was granted against the final digest. The model and context fields are recommended because they allow reconstruction, and they should not be presented to auditors as a quoted regulatory obligation.
- An approval should bind to a content digest, so that any later change makes it stale. For a client restriction change, this would let the firm show that the approver saw the same design and code that were released.
- Article 72 style requirements point to append-only storage with retained earlier versions, which supports the event store proposed in the second section. Git history alone would need protection against rewriting.

### Gaps
- DORA Article 17, the DORA Level 1 text, and all of EUR-Lex could not be read because EUR-Lex returned empty responses to automated requests. Legal or compliance staff should confirm these against the Official Journal.
- SOX sections 302 and 404, PCAOB AS 2201 and AS 1105, and SEC rules were not read in primary form. Statements above about ITGC are secondary.
